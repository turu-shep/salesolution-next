## 2. What the research says

### 2.1 What distributors buy first → what the install contains

The picture from the trade's own data (claims in §5, bank B):

- Technical buyers do **~66% of the buying process online before they'll talk to anyone** — and their #1 routine research source is the **supplier's own website** (41%), with **datasheets the most-valued content** (40% of all technical buyers; 56% among hardware buyers). The RFQ is won or lost before the phone rings. [B2, B3]
- Yet in exactly our revenue band, the surface isn't there: **only ~25% of $10–50M distributors can take a shopping-cart order at all** (35% at $50–100M vs 50% at $1B+). [B1]
- **45% of B2B buyers are dissatisfied with their distributors' online experience** — site search and spec findability among the named complaints. [B4]
- The ICP is **already burned**: only ~23% of distributors are very/extremely satisfied with their e-commerce ROI, and 23% are not satisfied at all. [B6]
- The competitive clock: distributors' own **AI deployment in marketing more than doubled in one year (22% → 48%)**, and **94% of business buyers now use AI in the buying process**. [B7, B8]

**Install recommendation (adopted in §1):** concentrate the install on the **pre-contact surface** — site/RFQ path + Catalog AI on the money SKUs + the AI-search foundation laid on the rebuilt catalog in the same engagement. Editorial and Outbound stay OUT of the install: editorial has nothing to cite until the catalog exists; outbound pointed at a broken site burns the list. This answers the brief's "concentrated and complete": every item is checkable, and it maps to a thing he can count — RFQs from a site that finally answers spec questions.

**First cylinder after install: Outbound Email.** Three evidence legs [B5]: demand in this sector is proactively pushed (field sales, CSRs, and outbound telesales are the top-rated drivers of e-commerce demand — DSG 2024); email is the fastest-rising rated channel (18% "most effective" + 16% second); and distributors' top financial objective is new customers in existing geographies — outbound's exact job. It also produces attributable quotes within weeks, which hands the retainer conversation provable gain numbers.

One honest gap: no current public stat exists for "% of distributors with no full-time marketer" (nearest: DSG/MDM 2020, only 3.3% self-rate as marketing high performers). Use the capability gap qualitatively; never invent a team-size number.

### 2.2 The leak model (the calculator this page doesn't have)

**Formula, in his units:**

> Annual leak = (RFQs/wk × 50) × avg quoted order $ × [close-rate points lost to slow quotes + share never followed up × recapture rate] + reorder attrition $

**Worked example — $10M distributor (all defaults GATE:HUMAN; sources in §5 bank A):**

| Input | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| RFQs/week | 60 | **Judgment** — pulled from HIS inbox/counter during the diagnostic; the model is designed to run on his numbers |
| Avg quoted order | $2,000 | **Judgment**, anchored between MSC's disclosed $392 catalog-order size [A5] and ~$1.8K B2B online AOV; quoted RFQ work runs larger than catalog orders |
| Baseline quote close rate | 25% | Inside the 20–40% manual-quoting band [A4 — vendor page, the weakest source in the bank; the diagnostic replaces it with his quote log on day one] |
| Close-rate points recovered by fast quotes | +5 pts | Judgment credit; direction from the response-time literature: ~7x qualification within the hour vs an hour later, >60x vs 24h+ [A1]; 100x contact odds at 5 min vs 30 [A3]; average B2B response is 42 hours and 23% of companies never respond at all [A2] |
| Quotes never followed up | 25%, recover orders on 10% of them | Judgment; direction supported by the 23%-never-responded audit [A2] |
| Reorder attrition recaptured | 2 retention pts on the base | DSG: 1 pt/yr retention over 10 yrs = +20% annual earnings ("for some companies"); below 90% retention, retention beats acquisition on return [A8] |

**Output: ~$650K/yr leak in revenue** ($300K slow quotes + $150K unchased + $200K reorder attrition) ≈ **$175K/yr gross margin** at the Census 26.9% durable-goods wholesale margin [A6].

**The unit decision (it matters for the fee):** model and present the leak **in revenue** — that's how he measures ("we're a $10M shop"), and it's the unit the architecture's fee rule expects. Show the margin conversion **voluntarily in the diagnostic** — an agency-burned owner will do that math himself; doing it first buys trust. Trade-press cover for the word "leak": DSG's own "up to 35% of all orders contain profit leaks." [A7]

**Where it lives:** the diagnostic and the Growth Call are the primary home (the Weiss stipulation moment). The on-page version (§6.2) is a three-line fill-in block — this buyer trusts his own quarter more than any slider. A calculator component is optional (spec in §6.2); if built, defaults above, labeled as defaults.

### 2.3 Anchors: which 2–3 carry the page

Ranked by "is this HIS money already moving, or a hypothetical?" (bank C):

1. **Amazon's take rate (NEW — the strongest anchor).** Amazon Business is past **$35B annualized GMV, 8M+ business customers, 97 of the Fortune 100** [C1]; Amazon's published referral fee on Business, Industrial & Scientific Supplies is **12%** (Tools & Home Improvement 15%) [C2]. In his units: **every $1M of orders that migrates to Amazon Business hands Amazon ~$120K a year, every year.** Four times the install floor, on the first million alone. Not a hypothetical hire — a toll booth on his lane.
2. **The unchased quote as margin already spent.** A manufacturing lead costs **$553 blended** ($691 paid / $415 organic) [C3], and B2B sales comp runs **7.9% of revenue** [C4]. A quote that dies unchased isn't a missed opportunity; it's ~$550 of marketing plus a comp share already burned. The install reframes as **recovering spend that already left the building**.
3. **One house account vs the whale curve (the emotional closer).** ~40% of a typical company's business is unprofitable while **20–30% of customers provide ALL reported earnings** [C5]; a 5% retention improvement produced >25% profit gain in Bain's financial-services example [C6, scope it]. A house account isn't 1-of-200 customers — it's a slice of the small pool funding everything. **Do not invent a dollar value for "one lost account"** — no distribution-specific figure exists publicly. Let him plug his own account in.
4. **The $300K growth hire — DEMOTED to a footnote.** It fact-checks fine (VP Marketing base $245,953 [C7]; Hadzima's 1.25–1.4x covers taxes+benefits only, overhead extra, so ~$300K all-in is conservative [C8]). The problem is psychology, not accuracy: this owner was never going to hire a VP of Marketing, so it's hypothetical money. Keep one line in the FGO comparison ("cheaper than the hire you keep not making"); stop leading with it.
5. **The $15–40K/mo agency stack — kept, qualified.** Real money, but only for the subset already paying agencies. Qualify it ("if you're already writing agency checks…") or it reads as a strawman.

### 2.4 Done-deal mechanics without a guarantee

Market context that says publishing the floor is right: **51% of enterprise buyers name transparent pricing their #1 unmet wish** [D2]; B2B buyers' comfort with large remote purchases keeps rising (**39% comfortable at $500K+ per order**, up from 28%) [D1]; and **60% of technology buyers regret nearly every purchase** [D3] — regret is the emotion the mechanics must attack. Precedents verified live (names stay internal, never in copy): published monthly fees with no-contract framing in B2B content [D5]; fixed-fee diagnostics sold as "you own the output regardless of what comes next" [D7]; diagnostic fees crediting 100% toward the big engagement within 90 days [D6]; month-to-month exits paired with tenure proof and "you take your accounts and data with you" [D4]; first-week partial refunds on satisfaction — never outcomes — plus named-single-operator accountability [D8].

**Two additions worth adopting now:**

1. **Staged install billing tied to the written punch-list** — construction vocabulary the owner already trusts (progress billing, punch list): e.g. 50% at signature / 25% at the mid-install checkpoint / 25% at the install-complete walkthrough, with "or 100% at signature, minus 5%" offered so the stage plan reads as accommodation, not doubt (matches architecture D8; exact staging GATE:HUMAN). He never pays ahead of evidence — a structural substitute for a guarantee.
2. **The named operator beside the exit clause.** "You work with the operator who scoped it. No account managers." Once real tenure data exists, add the tenure line ("clients stay because it works, not because a contract makes them") — **do not fabricate the number before it exists.**

Also confirmed: the week-4 work-shown promise is *ahead of* market practice (no named firm markets a week-N checkpoint) — keep it and say it plainly. The free diagnostic stays free; the paid-diagnostic-credit mechanic is held in reserve.

### 2.5 Legitimate urgency (the honest why-now)

The argument, one sentence per stat (bank E; the phrasing rules below are load-bearing):

1. Google now answers a share of searches on the results page itself: AI answer boxes went **6.49% of queries (Jan 2025) → 24.61% July peak → 15.69% by November** across 10M+ tracked keywords [E1] — show the pullback too; honesty is the differentiator with this buyer.
2. In BrightEdge's tracked B2B-technology keyword set, queries triggering AI results grew **36% → 82%** (article's widest window: Feb 2025–Feb 2026) [E2 — scope as "B2B technology queries they track," not all B2B].
3. When the AI answer appears, fewer clicks reach anyone's site: **8% of visits produced a click on a result when an AI summary was present vs 15% without** (Pew, real user behavior) [E3]; the #1 result's clickthrough runs **~58% lower** where an AI Overview is present (Ahrefs Dec 2025 rerun; correlational, informational queries) [E4].
4. The buyer is already there: **94% of B2B buyers used generative AI or conversational search in the buying process in 2025**, and twice as many named it a more meaningful source than any other — including vendor websites and sales reps (Forrester) [E5].
5. The citation layer is concentrated but still open: **the top 30 domains take ~67% of ChatGPT citations in a product-comparison topic** [E7], yet **~31% of Google AI Overview citations come from pages outside the top 100** [E8] — being cited is a separate, winnable battlefield from ranking. That is exactly the play for a DR-10-class site's clients.

**Phrasing rules (violations get the page dismissed by this buyer):** never "clicks cut in half" (Google disputed Pew's methodology; state the 8%-vs-15% panel finding); never "your traffic drops 58%" (top result, informational queries, correlational); never "cited once, cited forever" (58% of cited URLs appeared only once — the compounding is domain-level, via comparison/guide pages); Adobe's 1,200% genAI-referral growth [E6] is retail and off a tiny base — "small but doubling every two months," never a volume claim; **never use Gartner's "search volume drops 25% by 2026"** — a Feb 2024 *prediction*, not a measurement. Honest tension kept honest: TrustRadius 2025 found 72% of B2B buyers *encounter* AI Overviews but only 7% report using LLMs in the buying process — so the defensible frame is "AI-assisted search already sits in front of nearly every buyer, mostly on top of Google; pure-chatbot research is the smaller, fastest-growing slice."

### 2.6 The improved condition (Weiss anchor — this is what governs the fee)

What growth is worth to the owner beyond year-1 revenue (bank F):

**The multiple bands, verified.** Industrial distribution trades at **6.4x EBITDA at $1–3M EBITDA, 8.9x at $3–5M, 11.4x at $5–10M** [F1 — FirstPageSage Feb 2025; note: the report lives at `/business/distribution-company-ebitda-valuation-multiples/`; the URL held in the architecture doc 404s — repoint it]. Independent brackets: Peakstone (Capital IQ data) puts median EV/EBITDA on US industrials & manufacturing deals at **9.9x (2024) / 9.7x (H1 2025)** on much larger median deals [F2]; Capstone's middle-market index reads **9.0x (2023) → 9.4x (2024) → 9.8x (2025)** [F3 — note the 2023 base was restated between editions; say "rose across 2023–25," not "three straight years"]. The sub-$25M floor and the size premium come from GF Data's closed-deal set: **~5.5x at $1–5M TEV, 5.6x at $5–10M, 6.2–6.7x at $10–25M — "the size premium remains alive"** [F4].

**The worked example (the value-conversation beat).** NAW's benchmark: average distributors run **~4% EBITDA on sales; elite ones 8–12%** [F8]. A distributor growing **$8M → $15M revenue at a 10% margin** goes $800K → $1.5M EBITDA. The $800K company trades at small-deal money (~5.5x, GF Data): **EV ≈ $4.4M**. The $1.5M company enters the industrial $1–3M band at 6.4x: **EV ≈ $9.6M**. Same owner, same building: **≈ +$5.2M of enterprise value**, because earnings grew AND every existing EBITDA dollar re-rated into a higher band. At average 4% margins the same revenue growth is worth only ~+$1.5M — which is the argument for **margin-accretive growth** (house accounts, reorders, quote win-rate) over any-revenue growth, and it's also FGO's quiet pitch. For the larger ICP the arbitrage compounds: $3M EBITDA at 8.9x is $26.7M; $5M+ at 11.4x is $57M+ — the last $2M of EBITDA more than doubles the company. (Constant-margin math throughout; no operating-leverage credit taken — no citable figure exists, so none is invented.)

**The succession sentence (one line, verbatim-usable in the value conversation):** growth is retirement math, not marketing math — **75% of owners plan to exit within 10 years (49% within 5), the business is on average ~80% of the owner's net worth** [F6], and with **2.9M US businesses owned by people 55+ (more than half of privately held employer businesses)** [F5], he will be selling into the most crowded seller's market on record, where the growing distributor gets the multiple and the flat one gets the discount.

**Revenue quality is a second value stream.** A single customer above **20% of sales triggers detailed buyer review and can cut valuation "perhaps 20–35%"; above 30% many buyers decline outright** [F7]. New-account growth doesn't just add revenue — it repairs the multiple. This is also the honest counterweight to the house-account anchor: defend the whales AND dilute the concentration.

**The fee against the condition:** against the +$5.2M EV effect, a $30–60K install is **0.6–1.2% of the value created** — and 4–9% of the year-one EBITDA gain alone (~$700K), i.e. inside the Weiss 10%-of-gain rule while ignoring the enterprise-value multiplier entirely. This is how "$30K+" is made to read small without one hype word.

## 3. Fee mechanics for this vertical

The architecture's rule, applied with §2.2's model and §2.6's condition:

- **Install = max($30K, ~10% of the modeled 12-month gain), gain stated in revenue** (his unit — §2.2's unit decision). Worked: $650K modeled → **$50–65K install**; ~$300K modeled → the $30K floor; below ~$250–300K modeled → not an install client (sprint door or decline).
- **Year-one total ≤ ~15% of stipulated value.** On cash margin alone a mid-size account runs 3–5:1 — defensible, not "obvious." **The EV line (§2.6) must be in every value conversation** to make the ~10x arithmetic true without a hype word: recovered revenue → EBITDA at his margin → enterprise value at the sector band. The buyer stipulates the numbers; we supply only the multiple band, verbally, with its source and date.
- **The exact fee appears nowhere on any page.** It exists in one artifact: the written SOW, 48 hours after the call, value stipulated first. No stipulated value → no proposal; a "here's what we'd need to measure first" note goes instead.
- On-page grammar: floor + scaling principle only — "from $30,000, scaled to the value at stake. A $40M distributor doesn't pay what an $8M one pays." The *principle* is the trust signal ("they price to my situation"); the withheld total is what keeps the install out of comparison-shopping.

### 4b.2 "Grow it / keep it" (SOW copy content — all items already banked in §5)

- **Grow it:** new RFQs from a catalog AI can read [B2, B3] · quotes out the same day instead of 42 hours [A1, A2] · cited when a buyer asks ChatGPT who stocks the part [E5, E8].
- **Keep it (the erosion, in dollars — no statutes needed):** the Amazon toll — every $1M of orders that migrates pays ~$120K/yr, forever [C1, C2] · house accounts reordering from whoever emailed first [A8, C5] · customer concentration cutting the multiple 20–35% at sale [F7] · demand that dies with the owner's phone habits (the succession sentence, F5/F6). Keep-it names erosions grow-it doesn't already cover — that's the test for including any item.

### 4b.3 The transfer block (§6.4 objection #5, elevated into SOW structure)

Two columns near the SOW's close: **"Yours if this ends"** — product pages, RFQ mechanics, lists and sequences, code, reporting; transfers on 90 days' notice, no clawback — vs **"what the operating keeps in motion"** — reading the two lines monthly, moving weight to the cylinder that pays, the re-aim judgment. The pressure is symmetric to book-jobs' guarantee without borrowing its language: each month is a re-decision the report has to keep winning. FGO appears as Option 3 with its annual figure stated on the SOW (from $20K/mo ≈ $240K/yr) so the install anchors against the ladder's real top rung.

### 6.4 Five objection rewrites (sharpened for the $30K ask)

**1. "Why won't you guarantee results at this price?"** *(the big one — the absence becomes the reason to trust the price)*

> Because at this price, nobody honest can. A distributor's cycle is too long and too lumpy for a promised count of quotes by a date to mean anything, and anyone selling that promise has already priced the misses into the fee. So the trust runs the other way. Prices published before we ever talk. Your number in writing 48 hours after the call. Work on the table in week four. Two revenue lines on every monthly report, our fee as its own row. The door open on 90 days' notice, and everything we built goes with you. A guarantee asks you to trust our word. This asks you to read your own report.

**2. "Why does it start at $30,000?"**

> Because the fee scales to the value at stake, not to our hours. A $40M distributor with 60,000 SKUs and hundreds of open accounts is not buying what an $8M shop with one counter is buying, so they don't pay the same number. $30,000 is the floor, not a menu price. Your figure comes out of what the call and the diagnostic find, and it lands in the written SOW next to the math that produced it. If the value at stake doesn't clear the floor several times over in the first year, we'll say so on the call. An install that can't pay back isn't a sale we want.

**3. "That's a big check before I've seen you work."**

> Then don't start with the check. Pick the cylinder that leaks worst (the catalog, the AI answer, the cold list) and run it as a fixed-scope sprint at its published band. You see finished work on your site, moving your numbers, before the install is on the table. Go ahead within 90 days and the sprint fee credits 100% toward the install. Proving us out costs you nothing extra.

**4. "We've paid for marketing before. We got reports, not revenue."**

> You bought slices. The SEO shop proved rankings moved. The web shop proved the site shipped. Nobody owned whether a quote closed, so nobody reported it. We run the whole flow, and every monthly report splits into two revenue lines: what your ad spend produced, and what the engine brought back — recovered RFQs, reactivated accounts, reorders. Our fee sits on the same page as its own row. If the second line doesn't justify it, you'll see it before we do. That's the report you never got.

**5. "What do we actually own if this ends?"**

> Everything the fee built. The product pages, the RFQ mechanics, the lists and sequences, the code, the reporting — all of it transfers on 90 days' notice, no clawback. What stops is the operating: reading the two lines each month and moving the weight to the cylinder that pays. A retainer stops producing the day the invoices stop. An install stays on your side of the ledger, and whoever buys the business someday inherits it with the rest.
