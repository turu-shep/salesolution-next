# ChatGPT SEO: A Working Checklist for Getting Your Business Named

"ChatGPT SEO" is a slightly broken phrase — there's no results page to rank on — but everyone knows what it means: when a potential customer asks ChatGPT for a recommendation in your category, you want to be the recommendation.

That outcome is influenceable. Not scriptable — nobody controls the model — but influenceable, the way search rankings always were. Here's the checklist I run for clients, in order.

## 1. Find out where you stand (20 minutes, free)

Write down ten questions a buyer would ask before finding a business like yours. Not your keywords — questions. "Who makes reliable hydraulic pumps for food processing?" "Best dental practice in Mesa for implants?" Ask ChatGPT each one, in a fresh chat, with web browsing on. Log every business it names.

Most owners discover one of two things: a competitor owns the answers, or nobody does. Both are useful. The second one is an open door.

## 2. Understand the two ways ChatGPT knows you

ChatGPT names businesses from two sources. **Training data** — what it absorbed about the web up to its cutoff — which you influence slowly, through years of consistent presence. And **live retrieval** — when it searches the web mid-conversation and reads pages before answering. That second path is the one you can move this quarter, because it runs on pages that exist today and can be fetched, parsed, and quoted.

Which leads to the unglamorous part.

## 3. Make your site quotable

When ChatGPT retrieves your page, it needs a sentence worth lifting. Most business websites don't contain one. They contain slogans.

Go to your most important page and check whether these sentences literally exist: what you do, for whom, where, at what price range, and what makes you the wrong choice for some buyers. That last one sounds counterintuitive, but qualified, honest claims read as credible to systems trained to detect marketing fluff — and they're exactly the sentences that get quoted.

Then check whether a machine can even read it. Menus behind JavaScript, prices behind "call us," key specs inside a PDF — every one of those is content that effectively doesn't exist during retrieval.

## 4. Get corroborated

ChatGPT's retrieval cross-checks. If it finds "we're the leading supplier of X" only on your own site, that claim is weightless. If it finds your name in a trade publication's roundup, a local news story, a supplier directory, and thirty Google reviews saying roughly the same thing, the claim has legs.

So the off-site work: get reviewed (volume and recency both matter), get listed where your industry already gets listed, and get written about — one genuine article in a publication your buyers read beats fifty directory stubs. This is the slowest lever and the strongest one.

## 5. Answer the questions people actually ask

Take the ten questions from step one and make sure a page on your site answers each, directly, in the first hundred words. Then extend it: the comparison questions ("X vs Y"), the "is it worth it" questions, the "how much does it cost" questions your sales team answers by phone every day. Every question your site answers plainly is a retrieval target you now compete for. Every question it dodges is one you've conceded.

## 6. Re-test monthly, same questions, same method

Run the step-one test every month and log the results. Movement is slow and lumpy — in my client work, a brand-new industrial site took 12 weeks to earn its first citation, then compounded from there. If nothing has moved in four months, revisit steps 3 and 4; one of them is usually the blockage.

That's the whole game: measure, be quotable, be corroborated, answer real questions, repeat. It's not magic and it's not fast. But the businesses doing this now are being recommended by a channel their competitors haven't noticed losing yet — and if you'd rather hand the checklist to someone who does it daily, that's the work I do: helping businesses [get named in AI answers](https://salesolution.net/).
