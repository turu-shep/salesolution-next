# Pronunciation & accent — the sound layer

Source: a guide from your pronunciation tutor, 2026-07-21 (`docs/personal/Arthur_-_Pronunciation.docx`). This file is that guide, reorganized, corrected against standard dictionary pronunciation, and folded into the rest of the pack. Read this once, the same way as [07-how-and-why.md](./07-how-and-why.md) — it explains itself as it goes.

## How this is different from everything else in the pack (00–07)

Files 00–07 train **structure**: never restart a sentence, know your landing before you start, keep sentences short. That's the skill of *organizing a thought in real time*. It has nothing to do with whether the individual sounds coming out of your mouth are the sounds a listener expects.

This file trains **sound**: the actual phonemes, stress, and rhythm of American English. You can execute forward-only and land-first perfectly and still sound harder to understand than necessary, because "strategy" gets four evenly-weighted syllables instead of one stressed one, or "bad" comes out sounding like "bat." That's not a structure problem — it's a pronunciation problem, and it needs its own kind of drill: not "assemble a thought," but "say this specific sound correctly, on repeat, until your mouth does it without thinking."

The two layers run in parallel, not in sequence. You don't need to fix your pronunciation before working on structure, or vice versa — they're being trained by different parts of the same daily routine (see "Where this lives in your day," below).

## 1 · Vowel reduction (schwa) — not in the original guide

**The issue:** English does not give every vowel full value. Unstressed syllables collapse toward a neutral "uh" sound (written /ə/, "schwa") — the *fastest, laziest* vowel your mouth can make. Ukrainian doesn't reduce vowels this way — every vowel gets pronounced close to its full written value, stressed or not. That habit is a major reason Slavic-accented English sounds rhythmically "foreign" even when every individual consonant is textbook-correct — because English rhythm (section 4/8 below) *depends on* the unstressed syllables being thrown away quickly and quietly. Fix the vowel, and the rhythm the guide already asks for gets much easier to do.

**Target words — the schwa is bolded, say it as a throwaway "uh," not a full vowel:**
- ab**ou**t → "**uh**-BOWT," not "AH-boot"
- comp**a**ny → "COM-p**uh**-nee"
- cust**o**mer → "CUS-t**uh**-mer"
- opp**o**rtunity → "**uh**-por-TOO-n**uh**-tee"
- str**a**tegy → "STRA-t**uh**-jee"
- bec**au**se → "b**uh**-CUZ"
- tod**a**y → "t**uh**-DAY"
- supp**o**rt → "s**uh**-PORT"
- ag**ai**n → "**uh**-GEN"
- ann**ou**nce → "**uh**-NOWNSS"

**Practice sentence (also drills sentence stress at once):** "I think THIS strategy will help THE team." Say "this" and "the" as fast, thrown-away schwas — "th's," "thuh" — while STRATEGY, HELP, and TEAM keep their full vowel value and get the weight. That contrast — full vowels on content words, schwa on function words — *is* the rhythm the guide's section 8 asks for. It's the same rule, described at the sound level instead of the timing level.

## 3 · Final-consonant devoicing — not in the original guide

**The issue:** this is a different problem than the guide's section 5 ("final consonants aren't audible enough"). This is about *voicing*, not audibility — Ukrainian and Russian both turn voiced consonants at the end of a word into their voiceless twin: b→p, d→t, g→k, v→f, z→s. "Bad" comes out sounding like "bat." This is extremely common, very noticeable to a native ear, and — unlike most accent features — sometimes changes the actual word or grammar you mean.

**The flagship example, because it's a word you use constantly:** "close" the verb (rhymes with "doze," ends in a Z-sound — "I need to **close** the deal") versus "close" the adjective (rhymes with "gross," ends in an S-sound — "that was a **close** call"). Devoice the verb and you've accidentally said the adjective. On a sales call, "close the deal" needs the voiced ending.

**Other target words — keep the ending voiced, don't let it collapse:**
- ba**d** (not "bat")
- goo**d** (not "goot")
- bi**g** (not "bik")
- jo**b** (not "jop")
- brea**d**, brand → bran**d** (not "brant")
- lead**s** (the sales noun) → ends "-dz," not "-ts"
- deal**s**, goal**s**, call**s** → all end "-z" after a voiced sound, not "-s"

**Practice sentence:** "We need to close five leads before the budget review." Four voicing traps in one line: close (z), leads (dz), and budget's -d- mid-word all want to devoice under pressure — say the sentence slowly first, holding each voiced ending deliberately longer than feels natural, then bring it to speed.

## 4 · Sentence stress (from the original guide, unmodified)

English emphasizes important words and shrinks function words — this is the timing half of what section 1 (schwa) is the sound half of. The guide's example is a genuinely good one, keep it:

- "I presented the **REPORT** yesterday." (what did you present)
- "I **PRESENTED** the report yesterday." (what did you do with it — as opposed to just reading it)
- "I presented the report **YESTERDAY**." (when)

Same words, three different points, made entirely by which word gets the weight. This is also directly useful for the speaking program's land-first work: the landing sentence of a block should have an obvious, single stressed word — if you can't tell which word in your own landing is supposed to carry the weight, the landing isn't sharp yet.

## 5 · Pitch range — a small addition to the guide's intonation section

The guide's drill (say "That's interesting" as curious / confident / skeptical / enthusiastic / neutral) is correct and worth keeping exactly as written. One addition: Slavic speech generally uses a narrower pitch range than American English — flatter highs and lows. The fix isn't new material, it's turning up the same dial: when you do that five-tone drill, deliberately swing your pitch *further* than feels comfortable. What feels like overacting to you will usually still read as normal-to-confident to an American listener. This is also the direct fix for "clear accent beats fast native mumbling" (G5) and for sounding "persuasive and inspiring," which is one of the things you said you wanted from this whole program.

## 6 · TH sounds (from the original guide, unmodified)

Genuinely useful, keep as written. Two sounds:

**Voiceless /θ/** (think, thought, throughout, thoughtful, theory, method, healthy, something, anything, birthday) — common error: substituting an S (think→sink). Fix: tongue tip lightly between the teeth, push air through, no voice.

**Voiced /ð/** (the, this, that, they, those, these, there, therefore, although, rather) — common error: substituting a Z (the→ze). Same tongue position as above, but with voice buzzing.

Practice sentences from the guide, unmodified: "I think this strategy will improve customer engagement." / "Throughout my career, I've worked with international teams." / "Those decisions had a significant impact on the company's growth."

## 7 · American R (from the original guide, corrected framing)

The guide's diagnosis ("too much tongue contact," "rolled or tapped R") is right, but "weak R at the end of words" undersells what's actually correct: American English *does* pronounce R at the end of words like "career" or "world" — it's just never a tap or a roll. The tongue curls back without touching anything. The error isn't "too weak" at the end, it's the same wrong motion (tap/roll) everywhere, including the end.

Target words (guide's list, unmodified): career, research, resource, remarkable, around, world, marketing, report, requirement, priority. Sentences: "Our research supports the proposed strategy." / "The quarterly report exceeded our expectations."

## 8 · Connected speech / reductions — register correction to the guide

The guide says: use reductions (wanna, gonna, kinda, sorta, didja, wouldja) "naturally in casual conversation," avoid them in "formal presentations." That's the right rule for a general English learner — it's the wrong rule for how *you* actually use spoken English, because almost none of your spoken hours are a formal presentation. They're sales calls and client conversations: naturally-paced, professional, but conversational — much closer to "casual" on this spectrum than to a keynote.

**The corrected rule:** don't fight natural reduction on calls. If you over-enunciate every "going to" and "want to" at full value, you sound stilted and non-native, not more professional. Let contractions and light reductions happen at natural speaking speed — that's a sign of fluency, not sloppiness. Reserve deliberately full, careful articulation for exactly one category: the pack's `[verbatim]` blocks (C6 the audit offer, C7 the guarantee, C8 the terms) and any numbers, dates, or contract terms — places where a listener needs to hear the precise words, not the vibe.

## 9 · The /æ/ vowel (from the original guide, unmodified)

Genuinely one of the largest single vowel differences between English and Ukrainian. Target words: have, can, manager, strategy, analysis, planning, challenge, practical, actually, annual. Minimal pairs: man—men, bad—bed, can—Ken, sat—set. Sentences: "We have an ambitious plan for the next quarter." / "Our manager presented a practical solution."

## 10 · Minor, notice-only (not worth dedicated drill time)

- **Aspiration on p/t/k:** English puffs a little extra air on these at the start of a stressed syllable ("**p**roject," "**t**eam," "**c**lient"); Ukrainian doesn't. Contributes to a flat quality even when the consonant itself is correct. Just notice it; no drill needed.
- **"Dark L"** (the heavier L at the end of a syllable, as in "people," "well") vs. Ukrainian's lighter L everywhere. Small, low-priority.
- **W vs. V:** worth a glance if it ever comes up in feedback; usually already resolved at your level.
