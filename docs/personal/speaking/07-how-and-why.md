# How this works and why — read once before week 3

Everything in this pack traces back to four problems you named about yourself. If you understand the chain from problem → rule → drill, nothing in the other files will feel arbitrary.

## The problem being fixed (your own diagnosis)

1. **Mid-sentence aborts.** You start a thought, your inner editor spots a structural flaw halfway through sentence two, you restart — and the restart crashes into fillers ("e-e-e") and long unplanned pauses.
2. **Fine in writing, weak out loud** — especially complicated thoughts.
3. **Staircase wit.** The good thought arrives minutes late and goes unshipped, because being wrong out loud once taught you to hold fire.
4. Wanted: persuasive, leading presence in rooms; better memory for people.

## The core insight

You speak like a writer. Writing lets you compose and edit at the same time — delete the clause, restructure, try again. Speech doesn't have a delete key, but your editor doesn't know that, so it aborts sentences mid-flight and forces a restart. **The restart is the disease. Fillers are just the noise it makes.**

So the training doesn't fight fillers directly. It retrains the editor: composition happens *before* the sentence (pick the destination) and *after* it (repair in the next sentence) — never inside it.

## The four rules, and why each exists

1. **Forward-only repair.** Never abandon or restart a sentence. Finish it ugly, then fix it in the NEXT sentence: "Let me put that more simply." Why: listeners barely register a clunky sentence, but they always register a crash-and-restart. And every restart you allow rewards the editor's abort habit; every ugly-but-finished sentence weakens it. This is the one rule that directly attacks problem #1.
2. **Land-first.** Before speaking, pre-decide only the final sentence — the landing. Why: the abort happens when a sentence doesn't know where it's going. With a fixed destination, the route can wobble safely; you always have somewhere to steer a struggling sentence. It's also why keynote speakers sound confident: they know their ending.
3. **Silence over fillers.** A deliberate 1–2 second pause where the "e-e-e" wants to go. Why: filler is the mouth idling while the brain catches up. The same gap held in silence reads as *thinking* — it's higher status, not lower. Week 1 the pauses will feel awkward; that's the drill working.
4. **Short spoken sentences.** Speak like Hemingway, think like a strategist — complexity lives in the *sequence* of sentences, not inside one sentence. Why: long nested sentences are exactly what triggers the mid-sentence structural failure. Eight-word sentences are almost impossible to crash.

## Why a library of blocks (not "get better at improvising")

The uncomfortable fact: people who "speak well off the cuff" mostly don't. They do **live assembly of pre-built components** — the origin story, the opinion on X, the answer to "what do you do," polished over hundreds of tellings. What looks like spontaneous wit is usually a saturated block deployed at the right moment.

You will answer "what do you do?" hundreds of times in your life. Build that answer once, drill it until it's automatic, and two things happen: it comes out clean under any pressure, and your working memory is freed to do the actually-live parts — listening, reading the room, choosing *which* block comes next. That choosing is the real skill, and you can only train it once the blocks themselves cost nothing.

Target: 60–70 blocks over 12 weeks, across the situations you're actually in — identity questions, client stories, explanations, sales-call moves, transitions/repairs, opinions.

**A block saturates at ~30–40 reps.** After that it stops being training load and becomes your warm-up/benchmark material. That's also why the pack is paranoid about *what* you drill: whatever gets 30 reps becomes automatic, so the practiced version must already be the compliant, factually-correct version. That's what all the guardrails and canon-checking were for.

## How to read a block (the Landing/Beats format)

- **Landing** = the pre-decided final sentence. Fixed before the take starts. Rule 2, applied.
- **Beats** = the sequence of ideas, one per sentence-or-two. A beat list, not a script — because a memorized script crashes the moment someone interrupts, and sounds robotic when it doesn't. A beat list survives interruption: you know what comes next as an *idea*, so the words can be today's words.
- **Exception — the `[verbatim]` blocks** (guarantee, terms, audit offer): those ARE word-for-word, always, because a prospect who hears two different versions of your guarantee decides you're making it up. Everything else: beats.
- Tags (`[I]`, `[we]`, `[warm-only]`…) mark voice and context so a rep never trains a line into the wrong situation.

## How to do one take, start to finish

1. Read the block's beats once. Close the file.
2. Say the landing **in your head** (on hard days, out loud).
3. Hit record. Stand if it's phone material.
4. Speak the beats in order, short sentences. Stumble? Finish the sentence ugly, repair forward.
5. Land on the landing. **Stop.** Talking past the landing = failed take.
6. Ten seconds of nothing, then the next take.

Worked example — C2, the leak hook. Beats: "you're not short on leads, you're losing the ones you've got" → "the call that rings out while you're on a roof — that homeowner called the next guy." Landing: *"The money was already spent making your phone ring. The job still walked."*

A good take: "Here's the thing. You're not short on leads. You're losing the ones you've already got. A call comes in while you're up on a roof — it rings out. That homeowner? He just called the next guy. The money was already spent making your phone ring. The job still walked."

A take with a stumble, handled RIGHT: "You're not short on leads, you're — losing the ones that already, uh, came to you anyway. *Simpler:* the calls you already get are slipping away. A call rings out while you're on a roof, that homeowner calls the next guy. The money was already spent making your phone ring. The job still walked." ← ugly middle, zero restarts, landed. **This take counts.**

The same stumble handled WRONG: "You're not short on leads, you're — no, wait. Okay. You're not short on leads…" ← restart. Take fails, no matter how clean the rest is.

## Why the day is shaped 8 + 8 + 6 + 4 (+4 evening)

- **8 takes, new block** — the training load. Enough reps to install something new daily.
- **8 takes, maintenance** (2 × 4 older blocks) — spaced repetition. Blocks decay without it; two takes keeps a block alive cheaply.
- **6 takes, objection roulette** — pressure training. Objections arrive unpredictably on real calls, so you draw blind and answer cold: retrieval under mild stress, which is the actual live skill. Then you read the canon card twice and re-record once — the cold rep trains assembly, the canon rep trains the words.
- **4 cold drills** — transfer. Random topics prove the skill generalizes beyond material you've rehearsed. The month-2 tell that this is working: cold takes start scoring like rehearsed takes.
- **4 evening re-records** — same-day error correction on the day's worst moments, while you still remember what went wrong.
- **Review = best + worst take only, ONE fix written.** One instruction actually changes tomorrow's behavior. Ten become none.

And above all of it: **one real call outranks five practice takes.** The booth exists to make the live at-bats better, not to replace them.

## Why scoring counts restarts first

Restarts/min is the direct measure of the disease. Fillers are downstream — they fall on their own once restarts fall (that's why fillers still being high in week 1–2 is the *correct* order, not a failure). Completion rate confirms forward-only is holding; landing-hit confirms land-first is holding. That's the whole dashboard.

# The morning warm-up (15–20 min) — the actual routine

Two jobs: protect the voice (30 takes/day is a real vocal load — athletes don't sprint cold), and prime the land-first structure before the day's first real sentence. Do it in order; each step is gentler than the next.

**Stop rule for everything below:** nothing should hurt or feel scratchy. Warm-up is quiet and lazy, not performance. Hoarseness lasting >2 weeks = SLP appointment, per the sales-learning doc.

1. **Breath — 2 min.** Stand. Hand on belly. In through the nose so the belly (not shoulders) pushes out, then a long steady "sssss" out — aim 15–20 seconds without the chest collapsing. ×5. *Why: pauses (rule 3) and full sentences (rule 1) die without air support; running out of breath mid-sentence is a restart trigger.*
2. **Hum — 3 min.** Lips closed, teeth apart, easy "mmmm" — you should feel buzzing on the lips and nose. Slide the pitch up and down like a small siren, comfortable range only, quiet. *Why: wakes resonance with near-zero strain.*
3. **Lip trill — 2–3 min.** The motorboat: blow air through loose lips so they flutter, add voice, slide up and down. If your lips won't trill, skip to the straw — same effect. *Why: the airflow back-pressure lets the vocal folds vibrate cushioned. Warms your range without any pushing.*
4. **Straw phonation — 3 min.** Hum *through a drinking straw* (any straw): gentle sirens, then "sing" a lazy tune through it. This is the single best-evidenced vocal warm-up there is and the one the sales-learning doc prescribes. *Why: maximum fold efficiency, minimum impact — it's a warm-up and a reset you can also use mid-day after heavy call blocks.*
5. **Articulation & this week's sound focus — 3 min.** Exaggerated slow vowels (a-e-i-o-u, big mouth shapes) to open the mouth. Then this week's pronunciation focus from [08-pronunciation.md](./08-pronunciation.md) — say the target words once slow, once at speaking speed, in the practice sentence (with Claude: just say `sound`). Then one tongue twister in each language you'll speak today — EN/RU/UA as needed. *Why: this is where the sound layer (08) gets its dedicated minute — it doesn't get its own slot in the evening session, it lives here. And clear articulation at speaking speed is set by the mouth, not the voice — your G5 opinion block is literally "a clear accent beats fast native mumbling."*
6. **2 × 90-second PREP reps — 3 min.** Take one *saturated* (old, easy) block or opinion. Speak it as Point → Reason → Example → Point, 90 seconds, landing pre-decided. *Why: this is the bridge from voice to structure — the day's first two reps of land-first happen here, on material that costs nothing, so the habit is already running when the evening session starts. It also doubles as your long-term benchmark: same blocks, compare month to month.*

Then go live your day. The unsaid log and the evening session pick it up from there.
