## Body (Sanity `body` portable text)

Most B2B marketing frameworks read the same. Market research. Goal setting. Brand positioning. Tactical execution. Measurement. Adaptation. The shape hasn't changed in fifteen years.

The buyer has.

In 2026, the average B2B technical buyer asks Google a question and reads the AI Overview before clicking. They ask ChatGPT or Perplexity to compare two vendors before either gets a chance to pitch. They scan a product page for schema-readable specifications before they scroll the marketing copy. The path from question to purchase order is no longer "search → click → convert." It's "search → cited → considered → click → convert." Skip the cited step and the whole funnel narrows from the top.

A B2B marketing strategy framework that doesn't engineer for the cited step is a framework engineered for 2019. This guide is the version that works now.

For readers who want the summary first:

1. **Market Research + Demand Signals** — what your buyers ask, where they ask it, and whether AI surfaces are answering them already

6. **Measurement + Citation Tracking** — what gets reported monthly, what gets reviewed quarterly, what triggers a strategy reset

## Why a 2026 B2B framework looks different

Three forces pulled the framework apart and rebuilt it.

**One: the click moved inline.** Google's AI Overview now appears on about half of US searches, and on the kinds of questions B2B technical buyers ask — "what's the working pressure of a JIC fitting", "how do we integrate Salesforce with SAP", "best ERP for industrial distribution" — the AIO sits above the first organic result and answers the question without sending traffic anywhere. If you're not cited inside that AIO, the impression happens without you. Ranking and being visible became different things.

**Two: the citation became the conversion.** When a buyer asks ChatGPT or Perplexity "who makes hydraulic adapters for high-pressure systems", they don't get ten blue links. They get a paragraph and three citations. Being in those three citations is the new top-of-funnel. The buyers who click through after that paragraph arrive 30–40% more qualified than buyers who click a traditional SERP, because the AI already filtered out the noise. The cost is: half the click volume, twice the conversion rate, and a binary visibility outcome — cited or invisible.

**Three: the legacy toolkit stopped predicting revenue.** Keyword rank and backlink count used to be reasonable revenue leading indicators. They aren't anymore. A page at position 3 on an AIO-triggered query can lose 60% of its CTR overnight when a competitor's structured data wins the citation slot. Position is a partial signal. Citation share, schema depth, and AIO presence are the new leading indicators. The framework has to measure them or it isn't measuring anything load-bearing.

Every step below is built around those three forces.

## Step 1: Market Research + Demand Signals

The 2019 version of step 1 was *segmentation*. Who are your customers, what do they want, where do they hang out. That research still matters. It just stopped being enough.

The 2026 version adds *demand-signal mapping*. For each customer segment, you need three additional inventory items:

- **Top 5 buyer questions** in their actual words (not keywords — questions). Pull these from sales-call transcripts, support tickets, RFQ subject lines, and forum threads. These are the queries the buyer's day starts with.
- **Where each question gets asked.** Google search? ChatGPT? Perplexity? An internal procurement portal? A trade catalog? A vendor recommendation thread on Reddit? Each surface has different optimization rules.
- **AIO presence on each question.** Run the question against Google. Does an AI Overview trigger? If so, what sources are cited? This is the visibility baseline — and the only one that matters for AI search.

> **Common mistake:** treating search-volume data as demand. A question asked 200 times a month on Google but answered by AIO on every search has near-zero click demand. The demand is real; the click demand is gone. Plan for being cited, not for being clicked.

In Sale Solution's framework template, this goes on the Step 1 tab — five rows for five segments, with the question / source / AIO-presence columns filled in side-by-side. If a competitor is named in the AIO citation list, mark it. You're not just researching buyers — you're researching what the AI engine already thinks the buyer wants.

## Step 2: Goal Setting + KPI Architecture

The framework lives or dies here. If step 2 measures the wrong thing, every downstream step optimizes against a misleading signal.

Two-tier goal structure:

**Tier 1 — Outcome goals.** Revenue. ARR. Pipeline contribution. New-logo count by segment. These are unambiguous and quarter-grained.

**Tier 2 — KPI architecture (the leading indicators).** Where the 2026 framework departs from the 2019 framework. The leading indicators that actually predict outcome in an AI-search world:

- **AIO citation share** — across your target query set, in what percentage of triggered AIOs are you a cited source? Sample monthly. This is the new SEO KPI.
- **Schema completeness rate** — what percentage of your indexed pages emit the schema required for AI parsing (Product, Article, FAQPage, HowTo, Organization)? Inventory monthly via Sitebulb or a custom crawler.
- **Average citation position when cited** — if you're in the AIO, are you citation 1, 2, or 3? Higher position = higher click pass-through.
- **Qualified-lead inflows segmented by intent** — informational vs. transactional vs. comparison intent. The intent mix is the new funnel-stage mix.
- **Schema-rich-result CTR** — for pages emitting Product or FAQ schema, what's the impression-weighted CTR vs. non-schema pages? Tells you if the schema work is paying off.

The 2019 KPI set — keyword rank, backlink count, organic sessions, time-on-page — still has a role. It's the second-tier monitoring shelf. The leading indicators above belong on the first tier because they predict the next quarter's outcome.

Budgeting lives inside step 2, not as its own step. Set the revenue target, walk it back to qualified-lead volume, walk that back to required AIO citation share and schema work, walk that back to the resource pool (head count or agency hours) needed to deliver it. The template has a worksheet for this — a backward-revenue model that ends with the monthly content + technical-SEO hour budget.

## Step 3: Brand Positioning + Citation Stance

Positioning used to be a single question: what makes you different to a buyer? In 2026 it splits in two.

**What makes you different to a buyer** — this still applies. One sentence. Specific. Unfakeable. "The only operator-led GEO consultancy specialized in industrial e-commerce." "The hydraulic-fittings distributor with the largest in-stock catalog of high-pressure JIC adapters in the Southeast." Whatever it is, it has to be true and falsifiable. Buyers can verify; you can't fake it.

**What makes you different to an AI parser** — this is the new layer. AI engines are trained to surface authoritative, citation-worthy sources. They surface based on signals: structured data depth, on-page evidence (specifications, benchmarks, dates, author credentials), inbound citation patterns from other authoritative sites, and topical concentration on a narrow expertise area. Your *citation stance* is the deliberate decision about which topical concentration you want to own.

Three citation-worthy claims, each backed by proof:

1. **Domain claim** — what is the topic you want to be cited on? Not just "B2B marketing", but a sharp slice: "AIO citation engineering for industrial distributors." Narrow wins citations; broad loses to high-DR generalists.
2. **Evidence inventory** — what proof do you publish that makes the claim citation-worthy? Case studies with named metrics, specifications, benchmarks, expert author bylines, published prices, published timelines.
3. **Authority signals** — what external citation pattern reinforces the claim? Trade press mentions, industry directory listings, podcast guesting, conference talks, peer-reviewed contributions. AI parsers weight these.

The Step 3 worksheet in the template captures all three in a single page. The Step 3 conversation with your team determines whether you'll do the work to back them up.

## Step 4: AI-Search & GEO Strategy

This is the step the 2019 frameworks don't have. It's also the step that decides whether the rest of the framework matters in 2026.

GEO — Generative Engine Optimization — is the practice of engineering pages and content to be the source AI engines cite when they generate answers. It overlaps with SEO but isn't identical. The optimization unit is different: SEO optimizes for ranking, GEO optimizes for citation. The two diverge most on technical pages, FAQ pages, and product-spec pages — exactly the page types B2B buyers consult before a purchase.

A working step-4 strategy has three parts:

### Part 1 — Query inventory and citation goals

Take the top 20–30 queries from step 1 that trigger AIOs. For each, log:
- Current AIO trigger status (yes / no / partial)
- Currently cited sources (top 3)
- Our current presence (cited / not cited / mentioned in top 10)
- Our citation goal for the next quarter (be cited / improve citation rank / unchanged)

You can't optimize for AIO presence without first inventorying where the AIO is appearing. Roughly half the work is research; half is execution.

### Part 2 — Schema audit and remediation

The structural prerequisite for citation. AI parsers extract facts from structured data faster and more reliably than from prose. Audit your site against the schema types that matter for B2B:

- **Product** — for any catalog page. Must include name, SKU, brand, description, offers, specifications.
- **FAQPage** — for any page with question-format headings. Direct AIO citation path.
- **HowTo** — for tutorial, framework, or step-based content. Same as this guide — the 7-step structure here maps to HowTo schema.
- **Article + BlogPosting** — for any editorial content. Author, publisher, datePublished, dateModified.
- **Organization** — site-wide. NAP consistency, alternate names, sameAs links to verified social profiles.

A complete schema audit produces a one-line-per-page table: what schema is required, what's implemented, what's missing, last verified. Remediation is engineering work — not content work — and it's usually done in 4–8 weeks of focused effort on a site of fewer than 200 pages.

### Part 3 — Content shaped for AI parsing

The format that wins AIO citations is consistent: a direct-answer block at the top of the page (2–4 sentences answering the literal query), question-format H2 headings, short paragraphs (2–4 sentences), specifications presented as tables or definition lists, citations to external authoritative sources, and an author byline with credentials. This guide is structured the same way. Look at the H2s and FAQ section below — every heading is either a question or a noun-phrase the AI can parse directly.

If you're rebuilding existing content to win citations, the order is:
1. Add a direct-answer block at the top.
2. Convert paragraph H2s into question H2s where the section answers a literal question.
3. Pull specifications out of prose into tables.
4. Add or verify schema (FAQPage, HowTo, Article).
5. Add at least one citation to an external authoritative source per major section.

For our [deep dive on GEO](https://salesolution.net/generative-engine-optimization-basic-to-advanced/), see the full pattern.

This step deserves a dedicated quarter the first time you run the framework. Compressing it produces shallow schema, missed citation opportunities, and rework. If the gap between your current site and the spec above is wide, this is the step where the framework's value is created.

## Step 5: Tactical Execution + Channel Mix

The execution step has the most surface area in any B2B framework and the most rooms to get lost in. Two principles keep step 5 tight.

**Principle 1: Sequence channels by buyer stage, not by tool.**

Most agency frameworks default to "SEO, PPC, social, email, content" — five swimlanes, each with its own monthly plan, all running in parallel. The result is a framework that's hard to coordinate and produces uneven coverage by buyer stage.

The 2026 reorganization is to map channels to buyer stages:

- **Pre-aware** (the buyer doesn't yet know they have the problem) — top-of-funnel technical content, YouTube demos, industry-publication contributions. Channels: organic content, YouTube, trade press.
- **Aware** (they know the problem, comparing approaches) — comparative content, frameworks, calculator tools. Channels: organic search (where AIO citation is the win condition), LinkedIn long-form, Reddit/industry forums.
- **Considering** (they know your category, comparing vendors) — case studies, integration documentation, ROI calculators, free audits. Channels: paid search (brand + comparison terms), gated lead magnets, conference / partner referrals.
- **Deciding** (they're ready to engage) — direct-conversion paths. Channels: published pricing, calendar booking, sales outreach.

Each channel inherits a buyer-stage. Each buyer-stage gets coverage by at least two channels.

**Principle 2: Don't run more than 2 "test" channels at a time.**

Channel-mix decisions go in three tiers: primary (proven, scaled), secondary (working, sub-scale), test (unproven, exploring). Test channels each take dedicated resource and rarely converge in under a quarter. Running five tests at once produces shallow data on all five and clarity on none. Two tests at a time, evaluated at end of quarter, kept or killed.

For a Sale Solution-style operator practice in industrial e-commerce, a typical step-5 mix looks like this:

| Tier | Channels | % of effort |
|------|----------|------------|
| Primary | Organic content (blog + guides), Technical SEO/GEO work | 60% |
| Secondary | YouTube technical channel, LinkedIn authority | 25% |
| Test (2 max) | Industrial marketplace optimization (Thomasnet, GlobalSpec), Outbound email | 15% |

The mix shifts when a test channel proves out or proves out negative. The mix doesn't shift weekly because of a vendor pitch or a conference takeaway. Stability of the mix is the discipline; what *fills* the mix is the creativity.

## Step 6: Measurement + Citation Tracking

A framework with the wrong measurement layer optimizes against the wrong outcome. The 2026 measurement layer has three timeframes and three layers of data.

**Timeframes:**

- **Weekly** — channel-volume metrics. Posts published, schema deployed, qualified leads received. Operational signal: are we doing the work?
- **Monthly** — leading-indicator metrics. AIO citation share, schema completeness, average citation position, schema-rich-result CTR. Strategic signal: is the work moving the indicators?
- **Quarterly** — outcome metrics. Revenue, pipeline, qualified leads by segment, average deal size. Business signal: is the strategy producing the result?

**Data layers:**

- **First-party** — GA4 events, CRM, form submissions, call tracking. Trustworthy, slow.
- **Search-engine data** — GSC, Bing Webmaster, AI-search citation-tracking platforms (Brandwatch, AIPRM, manual SERP audits). Authoritative on what the engines see.
- **Competitive intelligence** — Ahrefs/SEMrush/Brandwatch citation share, share-of-voice on target queries. Tells you where you stand vs. the field.

The **monthly review** is the most operationally important meeting in the framework. It's where the leading indicators get inspected, where execution lag gets caught early, and where mid-quarter corrections happen. Keep the agenda fixed: 15 minutes on each leading indicator, 15 minutes on quarter-to-date outcome metrics, 15 minutes on what changes for next month. One hour.

For the underlying analytics-report mechanics (which GA4 reports surface which signals, how to read AIO citation data), see our deep dive on [the measurement reports that matter](https://salesolution.net/which-reports-indicate-how-traffic-arrived-at-a-website/).

## Step 7: Adaptation + Quarterly Reviews

Frameworks rot. Markets change, AI engines change, buyer behavior changes, the business changes. The adaptation step is the framework's immune system.

A quarterly review has four questions:

1. **What worked?** Specifically — which leading indicators moved, which channels produced disproportionate result, which content earned citations.
2. **What didn't?** Equally specific. Which tests failed, which content didn't earn impressions, which schema work didn't produce citation lift.
3. **What gets reallocated?** Where does the next quarter's effort move? What does the new mix look like?
4. **What changes about the framework itself?** Sometimes the framework needs an update — a step gets added, a KPI gets replaced, a measurement cadence gets tightened. Version the framework. Date it. Note what changed and why.

The quarterly review is also where the *framework version* gets bumped. The B2B marketing strategy framework you used in Q1 should not be identical to the one you use in Q4 if any real learning happened. Version 1.0 in January, version 1.3 in October. The version history is the evidence that you actually learned.

Without step 7, a framework becomes a static document. With step 7, it's an instrument that gets sharper every quarter.

## Worked example: a hydraulic fittings distributor

A composite example, built from three real engagements [VERIFY: confirm composite framing acceptable]. Call them Northern Hydraulics Supply — a Southeast US industrial distributor of JIC, NPT, and metric hydraulic adapters, $14M annual revenue, 60% e-commerce, 40% account-based sales.

**Baseline (Q1 2026):**

- 19 blog posts on generic SEO topics, none ranking on a transactional query
- Site-wide schema completeness: 38% (Product on most catalog pages, Article on most posts, almost nothing else)
- AIO presence on top 30 product/spec queries: cited in 4
- Qualified lead inflows from organic: ~12/month
- Average position on target queries: 47

**Step 1 — Market Research + Demand Signals.** Pulled 87 unique technical questions from sales-call transcripts and RFQ subject lines over 12 months. Mapped each to its primary search source: Google for 71, ChatGPT for 9, Perplexity for 4, internal procurement portals for 3. Ran AIO check on the Google subset — 52 of 71 triggered AIO. Currently cited in 4.

**Step 2 — Goal Setting + KPI Architecture.** Set 12-month revenue target: +35% (organic-attributable). Walked it back: required ~28 qualified leads/month from organic by month 12, up from 12. KPI architecture: AIO citation share target of 30% on the top 52 queries (currently 7.7%), schema completeness target of 90% (currently 38%), schema-rich-result CTR target of +25% vs non-schema baseline.

**Step 3 — Positioning + Citation Stance.** Buyer positioning: "The largest in-stock catalog of high-pressure JIC adapters in the Southeast." Citation stance: be the cited source on three topical concentrations — JIC specification questions, NPT-to-JIC conversion guidance, working-pressure compatibility tables.

**Step 4 — AI-Search & GEO Strategy.** This is where 60% of the next two quarters' effort went. Schema audit produced a 47-item remediation list. Built Product schema with full specifications on all 4,200 catalog pages. Built FAQPage schema on 31 spec-FAQ pages. Built HowTo schema on 12 installation guides. Rewrote 14 catalog category pages with direct-answer blocks at top.

**Step 5 — Tactical Execution.** Mix shifted from "everything-balanced" to: 70% effort on organic content + technical SEO/GEO, 20% on YouTube install demos (a Test channel that proved out in Q2), 10% on industrial marketplace listings (Thomasnet).

**Step 6 — Measurement + Citation Tracking.** Monthly review installed in Q2. Brandwatch + manual SERP audit for AIO citation tracking; Sitebulb for schema completeness; GA4 for first-party.

**Step 7 — Adaptation.** Q2 review killed the LinkedIn-authority initiative (1 lead in 12 weeks of effort), doubled down on YouTube install demos (8 leads in 12 weeks). Q3 added a new step-1 sub-process: review AIO citation winners monthly and reverse-engineer schema patterns.

**Result at month 9:**

- AIO citation share on top 52 queries: 31%
- Schema completeness: 92%
- Qualified-lead inflows: 38/month
- Average position on target queries: 12
- Organic-attributable revenue: +47% YoY

## Frequently asked questions

> Note for Artur: These are the FAQ array entries — emit FAQPage schema. Each question is one of the long-tail GSC variants in our cluster.

**What is a B2B marketing strategy framework?**

A B2B marketing strategy framework is a structured set of steps that takes a business from "we want more pipeline" to a documented, measurable, repeatable plan. The seven steps above — market research, goal setting, positioning, AI-search strategy, tactical execution, measurement, adaptation — are the 2026 version. The point of a framework is repeatability: anyone on the team can pick up the framework, follow the steps, and produce a strategy without re-inventing the structure.

**How is a B2B marketing strategy framework different from a B2B sales strategy framework?**

A marketing framework optimizes for *creating demand and qualifying it* — getting in front of buyers, earning citation, capturing intent, handing qualified leads to sales. A sales framework optimizes for *converting demand into closed revenue* — qualification calls, proposals, negotiation, closing. They share inputs and overlap in measurement, but the optimization unit is different. Most B2B teams need both, integrated at the lead-handoff point. If you have a sales framework but no marketing framework, you're a sales team with a content distribution problem. If you have a marketing framework but no sales framework, you're a marketing team with a conversion problem.

**Is the B2B marketing strategy framework template free?**

Yes. The Google Sheets template above is free and ungated. The filled-in worked example (Northern Hydraulics, with every step pre-populated) is gated behind a single email field as a lead magnet.

**How often should I review and update the framework?**

Monthly for the operational layer (leading indicators, channel volumes, qualified-lead inflows). Quarterly for the strategic layer (which channels to keep, what to test, what to kill, what to reallocate). Annually for the framework itself — does the step structure still hold? Has the AI-search step needed to grow into two steps? Version the document; date the version.

**How does AI search change the framework?**

It adds step 4 (AI-Search & GEO Strategy) and replaces the keyword-rank-and-backlink KPI set with AIO citation share, schema completeness, and citation-rank metrics. The buyer-stage logic in step 5 still applies. The execution surface — what kinds of content win — shifts toward structured, direct-answer formats with schema. The measurement layer adds citation tracking as a peer to traffic tracking. Roughly 30–40% of a 2019 framework still applies; the rest needs the 2026 reframing.

**What KPIs should a B2B marketing strategy framework track?**

Leading indicators that predict outcome: AIO citation share on target queries, schema completeness rate across indexed pages, average citation position when cited, qualified-lead inflows segmented by intent, schema-rich-result CTR vs non-schema baseline. Outcome metrics that confirm result: revenue, ARR, pipeline contribution, qualified leads by segment, average deal size. Skip the second-tier shelf (rank, sessions, time-on-page) for executive reporting; keep it for tactical diagnosis.

**Do B2B and B2C frameworks have to be different?**

The structural steps are the same; the inputs differ significantly. B2B research surfaces more questions per buyer and a longer journey (1–6 months typical). B2B measurement weights pipeline contribution and qualified-lead quality higher than traffic. B2B execution leans heavier into LinkedIn and industry-specific publications. B2C inverts each of those. The framework template above defaults to B2B inputs; the structure works for B2C with input substitution.

**SMART goals or OKRs for B2B?**

For step-2 outcome goals, OKRs are better — they force the team to articulate both the objective and the key results, and they're naturally quarterly which matches the framework cadence. For step-2 KPI architecture, SMART is fine because the indicators are inherently measurable, time-bound, and specific. Use both: OKRs for the outcomes, SMART definitions for each KPI.
